<div class="section-heading">
<p class="eyebrow">03 / FRAMEWORK</p>
<h2>From microscopic theory to effective descriptions</h2>
</div>

<div class="section-content">
<p>
A central theme connecting my interests is the relationship between
microscopic quantum dynamics and macroscopic effective theories.
Thermal field theory provides access to equilibrium properties and
correlation functions, while hydrodynamics describes the long-wavelength
evolution of conserved quantities.
</p>


<p>
  In the absence of external sources, energy–momentum conservation
  is expressed as
</p>

$$
\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0.
$$

<p>
  Here, $T^{\mu\nu}$ is the energy–momentum tensor. For an ideal
  relativistic fluid, it can be written as
</p>

$$
T^{\mu\nu}
=
(\varepsilon+p)u^\mu u^\nu
+p\,g^{\mu\nu},
$$

<p>
  where $\varepsilon$ denotes the energy density, $p$ is the pressure,
  $u^\mu$ is the fluid four-velocity, and $g^{\mu\nu}$ is the spacetime
  metric in the mostly-plus convention. Dissipative corrections extend
  this description to account for phenomena such as shear and bulk
  viscosity.
</p>

<p>
  The connection to microscopic dynamics can be explored through
  linear response theory. In an appropriate retarded-correlator
  convention, the shear viscosity is related to the low-frequency
  stress-tensor correlation function by
</p>

$$
\eta =
-\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}
\frac{1}{\omega}
\operatorname{Im}
G_R^{T^{xy}T^{xy}}(\omega,\mathbf{0}).
$$

<p>
  This Kubo relation illustrates how a macroscopic transport coefficient
  can be connected to the response of a microscopic quantum system.
  It also highlights the importance of correlation functions in
  establishing a quantitative link between thermal field theory
  and hydrodynamics.
</p>
</div>
</section> -->

<div class="section-heading">
<p class="eyebrow">02 / CURRENT DIRECTION</p>
<h2>Relativistic spin hydrodynamics</h2>
</div>

<div class="section-content">
<p>
My current research direction focuses on relativistic hydrodynamics
with additional dynamical degrees of freedom, particularly spin.
This area raises fundamental questions about how spin-related
dynamics can be incorporated consistently into the macroscopic
description of relativistic fluids.
</p>

<p>
  The aim  of this study is to understand the structure, consistency,
  and physical implications of spin in these
  extended theories of relativistic hydrodynamics.
</p>


<p>
  The study is motivated in part by the physics of non-central
  heavy-ion collisions, where the initial angular momentum of the
  colliding nuclei provides a setting for investigating the interplay
  between fluid motion and spin-related phenomena. The quark–gluon
  plasma produced at facilities such as the Relativistic Heavy Ion
  Collider (RHIC) offers a testing ground for exploring
  the consistency of such theoretical framework in various limits.
</p>

<section class="section" id="open-questions">
<div class="section-heading">
<p class="eyebrow">06 / OPEN QUESTIONS</p>
<h2>What remains to be understood?</h2>
</div>

<div class="section-content">
<p>
Several broader questions motivate my interest in theoretical
high-energy physics:
</p>


<ol>
  <li>
    <strong>Microscopic origins.</strong>
    How do quantum interactions determine the collective properties
    of matter at high temperatures and finite densities?
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Emergent behaviour.</strong>
    Under what conditions does a microscopic system admit an effective
    hydrodynamic description?
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Dissipative dynamics.</strong>
    How can transport coefficients and relaxation processes be
    determined consistently from microscopic theory?
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Extended hydrodynamics.</strong>
    What additional degrees of freedom are relevant to relativistic
    fluids, and how can their dynamics be formulated consistently?
  </li>
</ol>

<p>
  Addressing these questions requires connecting analytical calculations
  with the assumptions and limitations of effective theories. The
  objective is not only to construct mathematical descriptions, but
  also to clarify their physical meaning and domain of applicability.
</p>
</div>
</section> -->
